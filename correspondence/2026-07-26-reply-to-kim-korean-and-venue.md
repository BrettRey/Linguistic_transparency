## 1. Honorification, and why §4.5 now reports an open verdict

I couldn't keep the section's original claim, which had dative marking concealing subjecthood and the construction overriding it on two diagnostics at once. Three things got in the way, two of them yours.

Park and Kim (2022: 1504) introduce the diagnostic alongside the statement that "the case marker itself does not determine subjecthood." At 1505 you caution that honorification is a discourse phenomenon whose agreement pattern "often defies a grammatical relation," and *ton-i manh-usi-neyyo* has *-usi-* on an inanimate nominative subject, which you take to support Lim's addressee-honorific analysis; footnote 16 calls the subject-agreement assumption "not foolproof." And Yoon reports the stacking as marginal and dialect-variable, shows nominative stacking on locatives and temporals, and concludes that what it marks is major-subject status rather than grammatical subjecthood.

Together those remove the concealment the schema needs. If case marking and subject-oriented morphosyntax aren't coupled in Korean, dative marking hides nothing, and the construction is a non-instance by our own §2.3 test. So §4.5 now reports both constructions with both sources, states the qualifications, and leaves the verdict open.

There's a further wrinkle from the schema side. Case-stacking availability isn't a relation that reaches subjecthood through the NP; it's a diagnostic for the experiencer's disputed status, as are *caki*-binding and quantifier float. Honorification would be a relation if it's grammatical agreement and a diagnostic if it isn't. So Korean may supply candidate diagnostics without an established relation, which is the cleanest reason for the open verdict.

**Which is where I need your ruling, because your work points both ways.** You and Sells (2007) devote §3 to "Honorification is not agreement" and call the subject-verb agreement position "deeply misguided," treating honorification as privative and expressive rather than feature matching; Yoolim Kim and Findlay (2023) build on that. But *Syntactic Structures* ch. 14 titles its section "honorific agreement," resists a purely pragmatic account, and gives the pattern a grammatical core, with *-si-* ungrammatical on an inanimate subject (*cha-ka o-\*si-ess-e*) and spreading through auxiliary constructions. How do you now situate the grammatical-core discussion in the 2016 book relative to Kim and Sells (2007) and Kim and Findlay (2023)?

I should be careful about what the open verdict buys us. It makes Korean a probative stress test, since the procedure doesn't simply convert a non-English case into a positive. It isn't evidence that the test is valid, because an underspecified test would also return indeterminacy. §4.6 now says only the first.

And if you think a different Korean construction fits better, I'd rather have your judgement than my guess. What the schema needs is narrower than "a construction with an override": the relation has to reach the property *through* the mediator, and the language has to attest a concealing variant of the same construction type, where the type is fixed on criteria independent of the transparency effect. My instinct is that the answer involves case rather than agreement, and I noticed Park and Kim cites both Yoon (2007) on raising of major arguments and Levin (2017) on nominative-nominative stacking. "There isn't one, and the open verdict is the right result" is a perfectly good answer.

## 4. What changed in §2.5, because of your indeterminacy manuscript

Reading it corrected us on a point of substance. We'd been calling the daughter-level relation "syntactic-category transparency" and putting the whole relation at the daughter. On your hierarchy (p. 46) selection transparency sits at the parent across both verb classes, and what the attributional daughter adds is category flexibility. The section now says category flexibility and treats it as the marked sub-case of your wider selection transparency (pp. 36–37), which also squares us with the title of our joint note. I've taken attributional and evidential in place of our "the *seem*/*appear* type," and your six transparency effects are cited, with four of them holding at the parent. That last part helps: §2.5 claims constructional sub-type predicts a cluster, and your catalogue makes the cluster bigger than we'd claimed.

One thing I've deliberately left thin. *He was what they call dimwitted* doesn't by itself show the construction occupies a non-NP slot, since predicative position admits both NPs and APs. The text now claims only that the nucleus is an AP. If you have an example in a genuinely category-discriminating environment, that's the one to use.
