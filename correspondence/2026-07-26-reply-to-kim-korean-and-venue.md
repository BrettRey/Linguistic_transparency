**Why I couldn't keep the original claim.** The section had dative marking concealing subjecthood and the construction overriding it on two diagnostics at once. Three things got in the way, and two of them are yours.

Park and Kim (2022: 1504) introduce the diagnostic with the statement that "the case marker itself does not determine subjecthood." At 1505 you caution that honorification is a discourse phenomenon whose agreement pattern "often defies a grammatical relation," and *ton-i manh-usi-neyyo* has *-usi-* on an inanimate nominative subject, which you take to support Lim's addressee-honorific analysis; footnote 16 calls the subject-agreement assumption "not foolproof." And Yoon reports the stacking as marginal and dialect-variable, shows nominative stacking on locatives and temporals, and concludes that what it marks is major-subject status rather than grammatical subjecthood.

Put together, that removes the concealment the schema needs. If case marking and subject-oriented morphosyntax simply aren't coupled in Korean, dative marking hides nothing, there's no override, and the construction is a non-instance by our own §2.3 test, for the same reason English coordination is. So §4.5 now reports both constructions with both sources, states the three qualifications, and leaves the verdict open, naming what would settle it: whether R tracks grammatical subjecthood, which dative marking would misreport, or major-subject status, which it wouldn't.

I think this is the better section, and not only because it's more careful. §4.6 answers the objection that our schema smuggles in an English-shaped default, and an open Korean verdict is stronger evidence for that reply than a clean positive would have been: a test carrying an English default would have returned a confident yes. So the paper isn't stuck, and nothing here is urgent.

**The real question is for you, and it's an open one: which Korean construction should we be looking at?** I don't think I gave you a fair target the first time. What the schema needs is narrower than "a construction with an override," and it's worth spelling out, because it's the reason both of the constructions we tried came up short.

Two requirements. First, R has to be a grammatical relation that reaches the property *through* the mediator, rather than finding its target by other means. Second, the language has to attest a variant of the *same* construction type in which the mediator's own default hides the property from R. The second is what makes the test falsifiable rather than a relabelling of whatever we find; English coordination fails it, which is why the paper excludes coordination.

Honorification is doubtful on the first count, and here I need your ruling rather than my reading, because your own work points both ways. You and Sells (2007) devote §3 to "Honorification is not agreement" and call the subject-verb agreement position "deeply misguided," treating honorification as privative and expressive rather than as feature matching; Yoolim Kim and Findlay (2023) build on that. But *Syntactic Structures* ch. 14 titles its section "honorific agreement," resists a purely pragmatic account, and gives the pattern a grammatical core, with *-si-* ungrammatical on an inanimate subject (*cha-ka o-\*si-ess-e*) and spreading through auxiliary constructions. Those look like different positions to me, or at least different enough that I can't tell which one you'd want cited. Which is it now? If honorification is expressive, it can't be the R in a transparency case, since it doesn't reach its target through the subject NP. If it's agreement with a grammatical core, it can.

§4.5 currently reports the disagreement rather than picking a side, which is honest but not ideal in a paper you're a co-author of.

So: given those two requirements, is there a Korean construction we've overlooked? You'll see the space far better than I can. My instinct is that the answer involves case rather than agreement, since case assignment is structurally mediated in the way honorification isn't, and I noticed that Park and Kim cites both Yoon (2007) on raising of major arguments and Levin (2017) on nominative-nominative stacking, either of which might have the right shape. But I'd rather have your judgement than my guess, including "there isn't one, and the open verdict is the right result."
