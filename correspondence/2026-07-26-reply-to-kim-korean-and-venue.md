## First, one change to the schema, because the rest depends on it

The schema now types its parts explicitly: three variables (X, P, R), two participant roles that fill out X (a bearer of P, and a mediator whose default behaviour would have concealed it), and two evidential controls (the diagnostic through which R's behaviour is observed, and the concealing baseline the case is measured against). §1 works the roles through one case, and §5's two new tables assign them for every case in the paper, with the relational verdict kept separate from the profile verdict.

Two consequences you'll want to weigh. The distinction between a *relation* and a *diagnostic* is what makes the Korean verdict come out open rather than positive, so §1 below leans on it heavily. And requiring the bearer and the mediator to be *distinct elements* costs us the collectives case: *the committee have decided* is now a near case in §2.2, because the singular morphology and the member-level plurality are two representations of one lexical head rather than access across a mediator. Loosening "element" to cover representational layers would readmit them, at the price of the non-instance test's grip, since almost any property could then be said to conceal another representation of itself. That's a real trade and I may have called it wrong. If you think collectives belong inside, say so, because the argument for demoting them is mine and it postdates the version you reviewed.

## 1. Honorification, and why §4.5 now reports an open verdict

I couldn't keep the section's original claim, which had dative marking concealing subjecthood and the construction overriding it on two diagnostics at once. Three things got in the way, two of them yours.

Park and Kim (2022: 1504) introduce the honorification diagnostic alongside the statement that "the case marker itself does not determine subjecthood." At 1505 you caution that honorification is a discourse phenomenon whose agreement pattern "often defies a grammatical relation," and *ton-i manh-usi-neyyo* has *-usi-* on an inanimate nominative subject, which you take to support Lim's addressee-honorific analysis; footnote 16 calls the subject-agreement assumption "not foolproof." And Yoon reports the case stacking as marginal and dialect-variable, shows nominative stacking on locatives and temporals, and concludes that it marks major-subject status rather than grammatical subjecthood.

Together those remove the concealment the schema needs. If case marking and subject-oriented morphosyntax aren't coupled in Korean, dative marking hides nothing, and the construction is a non-instance by our own §2.3 test. So §4.5 now reports both constructions with both sources, states the qualifications, and leaves the verdict open.

The typing above then bites. Case-stacking availability isn't a relation that reaches subjecthood through the NP; it's a diagnostic for the experiencer's disputed status, as are *caki*-binding and quantifier float. Honorification would be a relation if it's grammatical agreement and a diagnostic if it isn't. So Korean may supply candidate diagnostics without an established relation, which is the cleanest reason for the open verdict.

**Which is where I need your ruling.** You and Sells (2007) devote §3 to "Honorification is not agreement" and call the subject-verb agreement position "deeply misguided," treating honorification as privative and expressive rather than feature matching; Kim and Findlay (2023) build on that. *Syntactic Structures* ch. 14 titles its section "honorific agreement," resists a purely pragmatic account, and gives the pattern a grammatical core, with *-si-* ungrammatical on an inanimate subject (*cha-ka o-\*si-ess-e*) and spreading through auxiliary constructions.

I don't take those to be in contradiction. The 2007 argument is against feature matching specifically, and a grammatical constraint on *-si-* needn't be feature agreement to be grammatical. But our case turns on the narrower question, which is whether either relation *reaches subjecthood through the dative NP*. If honorification is expressive, it doesn't reach its target through the subject NP at all, and the case closes as a non-instance. So: how do you situate ch. 14's grammatical core relative to the 2007 argument, and does either version give us a relation that tracks grammatical subjecthood?

If you think a different Korean construction fits better, I'd rather have your judgement than my guess. What the schema needs is narrower than "a construction with an override": the relation has to reach the property through the mediator, and the language has to attest a concealing variant of the same construction type, with the type fixed on criteria independent of the transparency effect. My instinct is that the answer involves case rather than agreement, and I noticed Park and Kim cites both Yoon (2007) on raising of major arguments and Levin (2017) on nominative-nominative stacking. "There isn't one, and the open verdict is the right result" is a perfectly good answer.

I should be careful about what the open verdict buys us. It makes Korean a probative stress test, since the procedure doesn't simply convert a non-English case into a positive. It isn't evidence that the test is valid, because an underspecified test would also return indeterminacy. §4.6 now claims only the first.

## 2. The partitive data, corrected

Two things in Table 1 were wrong, both mine, and the second one I got wrong twice.

First, the plural-only forms (*lots, bags, heaps, loads, oodles, stacks*). We had them taking partitive *of*-complements only marginally, with outright zeros for *heaps* and *piles*. Those zeros came from testing four complements: *day*, *afternoon*, *time*, *people*. A wildcard sweep returns 465 tokens across 374 distinct complements, and the frame is attested for every member.

Second, and this is the one I need you on. We starred *the remainder of time*. The string is fine, and `the rest of [nn*]` returns 2,820 tokens across 696 distinct bare complements: *the rest of society* 442, *of humanity* 248, *of life* 150, *of nature* 78, *of mankind* 77, *of time* itself 75. So I unstarred it and moved the finding into the pseudo-partitive column, on the grounds that *rest* and *remainder* accept a bare complement provided it denotes uniquely.

That was wrong, and your and Van Eynde's own paper says why. You define a pseudo-partitive as [N1 *of* N2] in which "N1 denotes a quantity or amount of whatever it is that N2 denotes" (2023: 271), and you treat the bare N2 as the *characteristic* property that distinguishes it from a genuine partitive rather than as the definition. *Rest* and *remainder* don't denote a quantity of anything. *The remainder of time* is what's left of time, not an amount of time, and *the rest of society* is the complement subset of society. So this subgroup has no pseudo-partitive use at all, the original `×` was substantively right, and what was wrong about it was only the ungrammaticality star used to justify it.

What I've now written: *rest* and *remainder* are complement-set nouns whose *of*-complement denotes a presupposed whole. With a determiner on the whole (*the rest of the money*) the string is a genuine partitive on both of your criteria. With a bare whole (*the rest of society*) it satisfies the semantic criterion and fails the syntactic one, so your two criteria, which coincide for *lot* and the plural-only forms, come apart for this subgroup, and the semantic one governs. The requirement is that the whole be uniquely identifiable, not that it carry a determiner.

Two things I'd like you to check. Whether that's the right account, and whether it already has a name and a source in the literature that I should be citing instead of deriving it from frequencies. And whether the consequence I drew for §2.4 is right: because the absence of a quantity reading follows from what kind of noun these are, their value on the partitive diagnostic looks partly categorial rather than an independent empirical correlate, so I've given it less weight in the cluster than the determiner and override behaviours.

One positive result came out of the same sweep. The determined frame sorts the plural-only forms by sense: it pulls in the literal homonym for every member that has one, so *stacks of the** is library stacks and smokestacks, *heaps of the** is literal and colliery heaps, and *bags* and *loads* are containers and cargo. *Oodles*, the only member with no literal homonym, returns quantificational hits exclusively. That gives the class a prediction it didn't have before, and it bears on your measure/quantity/collective classification, so have a look at §2.5 if you have time.
