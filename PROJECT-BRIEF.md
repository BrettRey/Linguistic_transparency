Yes. I'd select **one primary meaning** and several **controlled subsidiary meanings**, rather than trying to cover every use of *transparency* in linguistics. Otherwise the article will become a literature survey whose centre is hard to defend.

My recommendation is this:

> Treat *transparency* primarily as **mediated accessibility**: a relation in which some property, relation, or interpretation remains available across a grammatical, semantic, constructional, or representational mediator that could otherwise have made it inaccessible, opaque, or construction-specific.

That meaning is broad enough to include CGEL's number transparency, semantic transparency, constructional compositionality, typological comparability, and processing accessibility, but it is not so broad that it collapses into "clarity" or "regularity".

## The senses I would select

### 1. Transparency as mediated accessibility

This should be the article's umbrella concept. It lets you avoid beginning with extraction, barriers, phases, or islands, while still allowing such cases to appear as a restricted subtype.

The core schema would be:

> X is transparent with respect to property P for relation R when P remains accessible through X for the purposes of R.

For example, *a lot of* is transparent with respect to number for subject–verb agreement: in *a lot of tourists are in town* and *a lot of money is being spent*, agreement is sensitive to the complement rather than being fixed by the singular form *lot*. The *Cambridge Dictionary of English Grammar* explicitly treats fully grammaticalised complex determiners such as *a lot/lots of* and *plenty of* as "number transparent". ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][1])

This is the best anchor because it is precise, relational, and CGEL-friendly. It does not presuppose derivations. It does not require hidden movement. It begins from descriptive grammar: which part of the construction remains visible to which grammatical relation?

### 2. Morphosyntactic transparency

This should be your first substantive domain. It includes number transparency, but not only number transparency.

Relevant cases could include:

| Phenomenon                           | What remains accessible                          | Mediator                |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------- |
| English *a lot/lots of tourists are* | number of complement                             | complex determiner      |
| English *a lot/lots of money is*     | mass/singular profile of complement              | complex determiner      |
| collective NPs                       | notional plurality or collectivity               | singular NP form        |
| coordination                         | number/person features of conjuncts              | coordinate construction |
| Korean case stacking                 | grammatical and information-structural relations | layered case morphology |
| Korean scrambling                    | argument/discourse relations                     | word-order variation    |

This sense is attractive because it fits both CGEL and your broader interest in grammatical description. It also gives a clean route into Korean: transparency need not mean "a dependency crosses a boundary"; it can mean that case, topic/focus, argument role, or discourse status remains overtly or functionally accessible under rearrangement or layering.

I would make morphosyntactic transparency one of the article's main case families.

### 3. Constructional transparency

This should probably be the article's conceptual centre after the umbrella definition.

In CxG, transparency is not mainly about whether an operation can pass through a syntactic boundary. It is about how much of a construction's internal form–meaning organization remains available to speakers, learners, and analysts. Construction Grammar treats constructions as form–meaning pairings, and Goldberg's overview states that CxG takes the basic units of language to be such pairings. ([Princeton University][2]) Goldberg's argument-structure work is also directly relevant because it argues that constructions themselves carry meaning independently of the lexical items in them. ([University of Chicago Press][3])

This lets you discuss a graded scale:

| Type                                   | Transparency profile                                                |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Fully compositional pattern            | internal parts and arrangement largely predict interpretation       |
| Partly schematic construction          | construction contributes meaning, but parts remain interpretable    |
| Idiomatically constrained construction | construction-specific meaning partly overrides compositional access |
| Frozen idiom                           | internal parts have little current interpretive accessibility       |

This is where your article can do something interesting metaphysically. CxG transparency is not a single property. It is a **network property**: a construction is transparent to the extent that its parts, roles, semantic contribution, and analogical relations remain accessible within the constructicon.

I would give this sense major prominence.

### 4. Semantic transparency

Include semantic transparency, but divide it into two subtypes.

The first is **compositional semantic transparency**, especially in complex words and compounds. In morphology and psycholinguistics, semantic transparency is commonly treated as the degree to which the meaning of a complex expression is predictable from its constituents, or the degree to which constituents retain their meanings inside the whole. Bell and Schäfer's modelling work on English noun–noun compounds explicitly discusses transparency in terms of expectedness, constituent meanings, and compound semantic relations. ([Springer][4])

The second is **referential or intensional transparency**, as in de re/de dicto contrasts. This is worth including, but I would not let it dominate. It is philosophically rich, but it may pull the article toward formal semantics and away from CGEL/CxG unless carefully integrated.

Semantic transparency is useful because it shows that transparency is not just grammatical visibility. It can be interpretive accessibility: whether the parts of an expression, the referent of a description, or the semantic contribution of a construction remains available.

### 5. Processing transparency

Include processing transparency as an empirical grounding, not as the main topic.

Psycholinguistic work on morphological priming shows that semantic transparency can affect processing, though not always immediately or uniformly. Heyer and Kornishova, for example, report stronger priming for transparent nominalisations such as *paleness–pale* than for opaque ones such as *business–busy*, but only at longer prime durations. ([Sage Journals][5])

This matters metaphysically because it helps distinguish a merely analyst-imposed category from a cognitively consequential one. If some transparency profiles affect recognition, prediction, memory, or learning, then they are not merely convenient descriptions. But the processing literature also warns against treating transparency as one simple psychological variable. Different tasks and timings may pick out different aspects of transparency.

I would use this sense as a check on the article's realism: which transparency distinctions are reflected in actual language use or processing?

### 6. Typological transparency as comparative accessibility

This should be included, but with a warning.

Typology should not treat *transparency* as a universal category waiting to be found in every language. It should treat transparency profiles as **comparative concepts**: analyst-defined tools for comparing languages. Haspelmath's distinction between descriptive categories of particular languages and comparative concepts created for cross-linguistic comparison is directly relevant here. ([Zenodo][6])

For your article, typology can ask:

> Across languages, which kinds of information remain accessible through which kinds of mediators?

That question can apply to case, agreement, word order, clause linkage, discourse marking, complementation, and constructional inheritance. But the typological notion should be explicitly distinguished from language-internal categories. English number transparency and Korean case-stacking transparency may both instantiate mediated accessibility, but they need not be "the same category" in the grammars of English and Korean.

## The senses I would demote or exclude

I would demote **extraction/island transparency**. Include it only as one historically influential subtype of dependency accessibility. It should not define the article.

I would also avoid making **phonological or orthographic transparency** central unless the article is meant to broaden into literacy, writing systems, or second-language learning. Those senses are legitimate, but they will dilute the metaphysical question unless they are treated as analogues rather than core cases.

I would avoid everyday senses such as "transparent explanation", "transparent terminology", or "transparent pedagogy". Those are not useless, but they belong to rhetoric or pedagogy, not to the kind of grammatical/metaphysical argument you seem to be aiming for.

## The best package for your article

I would choose these four as the article's main senses:

1. **Mediated accessibility** as the umbrella concept.
2. **Morphosyntactic transparency** as the descriptive-grammatical core.
3. **Constructional transparency** as the CxG-theoretical core.
4. **Semantic transparency** as the interpretive/compositional extension.

Then use these two as supporting perspectives:

5. **Processing transparency** as evidence for cognitive reality.
6. **Typological transparency** as comparative-concept discipline.

That gives you a coherent article rather than an encyclopaedic one.

## The metaphysical framing

Given your interests, I would not ask:

> Is transparency a kind?

I would ask:

> Which transparency profiles are projectible, and at what descriptive grain?

That formulation is more Khalidian. Khalidi's view treats natural kinds as nodes in causal networks and emphasizes projectibility, explanation, and causal structure rather than essences. ([philpapers.org][7]) Applied to transparency, this suggests that broad *transparency* is not one kind, but local transparency profiles may be kind-like when they support reliable explanation or induction.

So the article's metaphysical conclusion could be:

> *Transparency* is not a single linguistic kind. It is a higher-order relational family. Some transparency profiles, such as English number-transparent quantifier constructions, constructional compositionality gradients, morphologically transparent word families, or Korean case/information-structure configurations, may be local special-science kinds if they are stable, projectible, and grounded in recurrent grammatical, semantic, cognitive, or usage-based mechanisms.

That conclusion is CGEL/CxG-friendly because it does not require a universal syntactic object called *transparency*. It treats transparency as a disciplined comparative and descriptive tool whose local instances may have different ontological standings.

## A possible thesis statement

You might pitch the article around a thesis like this:

> Linguistic transparency is best understood not as a unitary syntactic property but as a family of mediated accessibility relations. A construction, expression, or grammatical configuration is transparent when information that might otherwise be encapsulated remains available for agreement, interpretation, constructional generalisation, processing, or cross-linguistic comparison. The resulting category is not a natural kind at the umbrella level, but some transparency profiles are projectible local kinds in Khalidi's sense: they are grounded in recurrent causal networks involving grammar, usage, cognition, and conventional form–meaning pairings.

That would let number transparency in English serve as the opening example, not the topic. The topic would be the wider metaphysics of mediated accessibility.
