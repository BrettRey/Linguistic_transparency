\section{Form--meaning transparency}\label{sec:3}

Section 2's relation was agreement. The schema isn't confined to it. Where $R$ is interpretation, learning, or processing rather than agreement, the same question (does $P$ remain accessible through $X$ when $X$ might have concealed it?) reaches a second family: the form--meaning transparency of constructions, compounds, and morphologically complex words. The relation is broader and the accessibility is gradient rather than class-like, but the slots bind as before. This section makes the case briefly; the form--meaning family is developed in its own right in separate work.

Three windows make the point, at three levels of structure: constructions, noun-noun compounds, and morphologically complex words. Take constructions first. Construction Grammar treats constructions as learned form--meaning pairings \autocite{goldberg-2006-constructions-at-work}, and their compositional transparency runs a gradient: from fully compositional patterns, through partly schematic and idiomatically constrained constructions, to frozen idioms like \mention{kick the bucket}, where the parts' meanings no longer reach interpretation through the whole. The frozen idiom isn't an exclusion but the trivial limit at the gradient's opaque end (full compositionality, where a mediator's parts predict the whole, is the transparent limit): the construction-type admits a concealing variant, so it sits on the scale rather than off it (contrast coordination, §\ref{sec:2-3}, whose type admits no concealing variant on the analysis adopted there). A plausible strengthening is that transparency is partly a \mention{network} property, a construction's compositional reading being accessible partly because related constructions in the \term{constructicon}, the structured inventory of a language's constructions, support it \autocite{kay-fillmore-1999-whats-x-doing-y}; that is a hypothesis, not a result.

The other two windows, at the compound and word levels, are quantitative and give the gradient empirical content. Bell and Schäfer \autocite*{bell-schafer-2016-modelling-transparency} model the rated transparency of English noun-noun compounds from corpus-statistical predictors (constituent frequency, semantic-relation expectedness, head productivity), so a compound's parts are accessible to a degree that tracks its position in the network of compounds. Heyer and Kornishova \autocite*{heyer-kornishova-2018-priming-eventually} find that rated transparency modulates masked morphological priming, but only at long stimulus-onset asynchronies: an early, semantically blind decomposition stage gives way to a later transparency-sensitive one. Their materials are deadjectival nominalizations in two languages: English \mention{-ness} (\mention{darkness} `state of being dark') and Russian \mention{-ost'} (\mention{blednost'} `paleness', from the adjective stem \mention{bledn-} `pale'), both highly productive and mostly transparent but each shading into idiosyncrasy at the opaque edge (\mention{business}, not `state of being busy'; \mention{novost'}, `a piece of news' rather than `newness'). (\textcite{feldman-etal-2015-must-analysis} contest the serial reading; the dispute is live.)

These three windows are three within-domain profiles (construction-level, compound-level, word-level), distinct in items, methods, and stabilizers (constructional inheritance, usage entrenchment, processing architecture), and each licenses within-domain projection. Whether they're joint reflexes of a single underlying organization (a genuine cross-domain cluster) or three separate profiles is an open empirical question. Their composition hasn't been established (§\ref{sec:7-2}), and a within-items probe would test it; that probe and the family's fuller development are taken up in separate work. Here the windows do one job: they show the schema's $R$ isn't fixed. A construction can be transparent for one relation and opaque for another. \mention{Kick the bucket} is opaque for interpretation yet morphosyntactically regular, its verb agreeing with its subject in the ordinary way; the number-transparent NPs of §\ref{sec:2} are constructions too, transparent for agreement whatever their interpretive compositionality. Accessibility isn't transitive across $R$-values. That $R$-relativity is built into the umbrella, and it's a feature: the schema asks whether $P$ remains accessible \mention{for the purposes of} a specified $R$, not in general. The umbrella analysis is also framework-neutral here, since \textit{CGEL}, Sign-Based Construction Grammar, and Goldbergian CxG encode the override differently and the schema sits above the choice.
