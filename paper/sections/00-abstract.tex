Linguists in several subfields call constructions \term{transparent} (information remains available through a structure that could have hidden it) and mean different things by the label: number transparency in NP agreement, constructional compositionality (how a construction's parts contribute to its meaning), semantic transparency in compounds, and transparent free relatives. This paper treats those uses as one family of mediated accessibility relations (access to a property through an intervening element) and supplies a test for when the label is earning its keep. A configuration is transparent with respect to a property for a relation when the property, borne by one element inside the configuration, remains accessible to that relation despite another element whose own default behaviour would have concealed it. The distinctive move is a non-instance requirement: a case counts only where the mediator's default could have done the concealing, which excludes feature summation such as coordination, and with it the temptation to relabel familiar facts as access. The schema is a comparative concept in Haspelmath's sense rather than a claim that languages share a descriptive category.

The worked cases are English number-transparent quantificational nouns (nouns whose complements can control number agreement) and transparent free relatives. Spanish and Tsez extend the same outward-endpoint pattern; Aleut, Lardil, Kayardild, and Chamorro pressure-test the direction and distribution of the relation; and Algonquian/Kutenai obviation marks the boundary where an intervening head acquires a feature of its own, so the relation tracks that rather than the bearer's. Form--meaning accessibility, including constructional compositionality, compound transparency, and morphological priming, extends the schema beyond syntax, with cross-domain integration left as an open programme.

The result is a division of labour. The umbrella label predicts nothing on its own; particular transparency profiles do, and the paper says which ones, what each licenses, and what holds it in place. The pay-off for descriptive and theoretical grammar is a way to tell a transparency label that carries predictions from one that restates the facts it was coined for.
