Linguists use \term{transparency} for cases in which a relation can access a property through material that could have hidden it. This paper calls the narrower relation \term{access transparency} and defines it as a comparative concept. An application requires an independently diagnosable bearer of the property, a distinct mediator, evidence that the relation tracks the property, and an independently established counterpart in the same constructional family that can conceal it. The protocol returns positive, non-instance, or indeterminate verdicts. It keeps mediated access apart from feature summation, whole-configuration construal, and cases in which the relevant bearer lies outside the configuration.

The paper demonstrates the concept with two grammatical cases. English number-transparent quantificational nouns provide the main empirical profile: their agreement override and constructional contrasts are well established, while a corpus pilot supplies partial prospective support and a feasibility failure for candidate-member discovery. Transparent free relatives provide a second, conditional grammatical illustration. A working verb-class check supports the proposed attributional/evidential contrast, but the verdict depends on the constructional analysis and remains pilot-level. Spanish and Tsez provide controlled typological extensions; the Appendix~\ref{sec:B} cases mark further hypotheses and boundary conditions.

Projectibility is a secondary question. The comparative concept predicts nothing by itself; a language-particular profile may support projection when an observable base covaries with further behaviour and an identifiable stabilizer supports that covariance. Form--meaning examples, including constructional compositionality, compound ratings, and morphological priming, mark the schema's scope rather than supply additional demonstrated profiles. The result is a disciplined way to distinguish a relational analysis that earns its keep from a label that merely restates the facts it describes.
