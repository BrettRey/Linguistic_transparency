\section{Introduction}\label{sec:1}

The initial puzzle is that some grammatical relations can still access a property through a structure that appears, on the surface, to block or reverse that access. Two examples from Huddleston and Pullum's \textit{Cambridge Grammar of the English Language} (\textit{CGEL}, \citeyear{huddleston-pullum-2002}) make the point visible (§18.2, p.~502).

\ea\label{ex:cgel-two-direction}
\ea \mention{A number of spots have}/\ungram{\mention{has}} \mention{appeared.}
\ex \mention{Heaps of money has}/\ungram{\mention{have}} \mention{been spent.}
\z
\z

In the first example, the surface head \mention{number} is singular, but agreement is plural because the \mention{of}-complement \mention{spots} is plural count. In the second, the surface head \mention{heaps} is plural, but agreement is singular because the \mention{of}-complement \mention{money} is non-count. The construction lets the complement's number/count profile reach the verb, even though the surface head's own morphological number points the other way.

\textit{CGEL} calls these \term{number-transparent quantificational nouns} (QNs).\footnote{\term{Quantificational noun} is \textit{CGEL}'s label, and the paper keeps it. In the pseudo-partitive literature the same nouns are \term{quantifier nouns}, one of five sub-types alongside measure, container, part, and collective nouns \autocite[271]{van-eynde-kim-2023-pseudo-partitives}, following \textcite[109]{keizer-2007-english-noun-phrase}. Van Eynde and Kim print \enquote{collection} for this last type; the paper standardizes on \enquote{collective}, following \textcite{kim-2026-form-function}.} Core members include \mention{lot, plenty, lots, bags, heaps, loads, oodles, stacks, remainder, rest, number,} and \mention{couple}, with \mention{majority} and \mention{minority} at the boundary. They head NPs whose agreement override is, on \textit{CGEL}'s analysis, the default, unlike the optional override of ordinary collectives like \mention{team} and \mention{family}. The narrower relation studied here is \term{access transparency}: access to a property through a mediator when an independently established counterpart shows how that mediator could have concealed it. The schema is:
\begin{schemabox}
\textbf{Umbrella schema.}\par\smallskip
$X$ is \term{access-transparent} with respect to property $P$ for relation $R$ when $P$, borne by one element within $X$, remains accessible to $R$ across a distinct mediator, while an independently established counterpart in the same constructional family shows how the mediator or the configuration could instead control $R$.
\end{schemabox}

The schema has three variables ($X$, $P$, $R$), two participant roles that fill out $X$ (bearer and mediator), and two controls (the diagnostic through which $R$'s behaviour is observed, and the concealing counterpart against which the case is measured). Keeping those apart is what stops a case study from drifting. Applied to (\ref{ex:cgel-two-direction}a):

\begin{quote}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{0.66\linewidth}@{}}
\textit{Configuration} ($X$) & \mention{a number of spots}: a number-transparent quantificational noun plus its \mention{of}-complement \\
\textit{Property} ($P$)      & plural count profile \\
\textit{Bearer}              & \mention{spots}, the \mention{of}-complement \\
\textit{Mediator}            & \mention{number}, the morphologically singular head \\
\textit{Relation} ($R$)      & subject--verb agreement \\
\textit{Diagnostic}          & the number realized on \mention{have} \\
\textit{Concealing counterpart} & head-driven agreement: \mention{the number of spots has appeared} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{quote}

Three distinctions keep the application disciplined. The property is not its bearer: $P$ is the plural count profile, not the word \mention{spots}. The relation is not the diagnostic that detects it: agreement is the relation, and verb morphology is how we see it. The counterpart is a constructional alternative established without appealing to the access effect under assessment.

The application proceeds through four gates:

\begin{enumerate}
\item
  \textit{Independent bearer}: $P$ is diagnosable on an element inside $X$ without using $R$.
\item
  \textit{Distinct mediator}: the bearer and mediator are distinct expressions or independently motivated structural positions.
\item
  \textit{Tracking}: the observed relation demonstrably tracks $P$, rather than a newly constructed property of $X$ or of the mediator.
\item
  \textit{Independent counterpart}: the constructional family is individuated without reference to access transparency, and it contains an attested counterpart in which $R$ tracks the mediator or $X$ instead. The counterpart may be a strong default or a weaker, dispreferred option, but its evidential strength should be stated.
\end{enumerate}

Coordination fails the second gate: in \mention{Bob and Jane are happy}, plural agreement sums the conjuncts' features, and no mediator stands between a bearer and the relation. Deferred reference fails the first: the relevant referent lies outside the subject expression. A case that passes all four gates receives a positive relational verdict. A case that fails a gate receives a non-instance verdict when the failure is established; where the constructional analysis or counterpart remains unsettled, the verdict is indeterminate. The protocol is an applicability test, not a falsification condition. Profile predictions can be falsified only after a positive application has been established.

This non-instance requirement is the paper's distinctive contribution. It keeps mediated accessibility from collapsing into a relabeling of familiar facts, while the three-way verdict records when an analysis hasn't yet earned a binary answer. Everyday senses of \mention{transparent prose} or \mention{transparent intentions} remain outside the schema because its slots have no values to bind.

Passing the applicability test is necessary, not sufficient, for projection. A label can identify a genuine access relation and still add no predictive content beyond the facts that motivated it. The secondary question is whether a positive case supports \term{projectibility} (what observing some features licenses us to predict about others; \citealp{Goodman1955}). A \term{projectibility profile} has an observable base, a set of predictions that base licenses, and a \term{stabilizer} that helps maintain the covariance. \textcite{boyd1991} supplies one maintenance-based account of stability. The QN class shows the shape concretely. Its determiner frames and complement restrictions support a candidate profile beyond the agreement contrast, but this paper directly audits only selected behaviours. The label \term{access transparency} itself projects nothing; any projection belongs to a language-particular profile, and projectibility is field-relative: a profile supports induction for a field's purposes, not for every inquiry at once.

The paper asks two linked questions: which configurations satisfy the access-transparent relation, and which positive cases support projection beyond the facts that motivated the label? The primary field is syntax, where the targets include agreement, syntactic-category licensing, complement selection, and behaviour under complement ellipsis. What this buys descriptive and theoretical grammar, and typology, is a way to distinguish an access relation from the profile-level question of what it predicts. The philosophy of science is instrumental: Goodman's projectibility, Slater's stable property clusters, and Khalidi's causal networks enter only where they change a grammatical assessment. Readers interested in the primary demonstrations can focus on §\ref{sec:2} and §\ref{sec:6}; §\ref{sec:7} supplies the shorter secondary profile assessment.

Our aim is constructive rather than exhaustive. The paper leaves aside most uses of \term{transparency} in linguistics: extraction, islands, barriers, and phases; phonological and orthographic transparency; everyday talk of \mention{transparent explanation}; and referential or \mention{de re}/\mention{de dicto} transparency, where the mediator is an intensional context rather than a form with internal structure, so the schema's \mention{through $X$} clause has nothing to bind to.

\term{Mediated accessibility} is more specific than \term{clarity} and broader than \term{compositionality}. Clarity is rhetorical: it doesn't single out a mediator or relation. Compositionality is the limiting case of form--meaning transparency, where a mediator's parts predict the whole. Number transparency is different: a QN's morphological number doesn't by itself predict the verb's number, and under the construction it doesn't have to.

Section 2 takes up morphosyntactic transparency, with number-transparent QNs as the main case and transparent free relatives as a second, conditional illustration. Section 3 briefly marks form--meaning accessibility (constructional compositionality \autocite{goldberg-2006-constructions-at-work}, rated compound transparency \autocite{bell-schafer-2016-modelling-transparency}, and time-sensitive lexical processing \autocite{heyer-kornishova-2018-priming-eventually}) as a boundary application where $R$ shifts from agreement to interpretation or processing. Section 4 applies Haspelmath's \autocite*{haspelmath-2010-comparative-concepts} comparative-concept discipline to the schema's cross-linguistic ambitions, with Spanish and Tsez as controlled cases and the fuller topology survey in Appendix~\ref{sec:B}. Section 5 gives the secondary profile assessment, using Slater's stable property cluster (SPC) framework \autocite{slater-2015-natural-kindness} and Khalidi's causal-network account \autocite{khalidi-2018-causal-networks} only where they sharpen the evidential verdict.
