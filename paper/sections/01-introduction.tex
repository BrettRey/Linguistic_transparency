\section{Introduction}\label{sec:1}

The initial puzzle is that some grammatical relations can still access a property through a structure that appears, on the surface, to block or reverse that access. Two examples from Huddleston and Pullum's \textit{Cambridge Grammar of the English Language} (\textit{CGEL}, \citeyear{huddleston-pullum-2002}) make the point visible (§18.2, p.~502).

\ea\label{ex:cgel-two-direction}
\ea \mention{A number of spots have}/\ungram{\mention{has}} \mention{appeared.}
\ex \mention{Heaps of money has}/\ungram{\mention{have}} \mention{been spent.}
\z
\z

In the first example, the surface head \mention{number} is singular, but agreement is plural because the \mention{of}-complement \mention{spots} is plural count. In the second, the surface head \mention{heaps} is plural, but agreement is singular because the \mention{of}-complement \mention{money} is non-count. The construction lets the complement's number/count profile reach the verb, even though the surface head's own morphological number points the other way.

\textit{CGEL} calls these \term{number-transparent quantificational nouns} (QNs).\footnote{\term{Quantificational noun} is \textit{CGEL}'s label, and the paper keeps it. In the pseudo-partitive literature the same nouns are \term{quantifier nouns}, one of five sub-types alongside measure, container, part, and collective nouns \autocite[271]{van-eynde-kim-2023-pseudo-partitives}, following \textcite[109]{keizer-2007-english-noun-phrase}. Van Eynde and Kim print \enquote{collection} for this last type; the paper standardizes on \enquote{collective}, following \textcite{kim-2026-form-function}.} Core members include \mention{lot, plenty, lots, bags, heaps, loads, oodles, stacks, remainder, rest, number,} and \mention{couple}, with \mention{majority} and \mention{minority} at the boundary. They head NPs whose agreement override is, on \textit{CGEL}'s analysis, the default, unlike the optional override of ordinary collectives like \mention{team} and \mention{family}. The wider question is whether comparable \mention{transparency} labels pick out real accessibility relations or only restate the facts they describe. The umbrella schema is:
\begin{schemabox}
\textbf{Umbrella schema.}\par\smallskip
$X$ is \mention{transparent} with respect to property $P$ for relation $R$ when $P$, borne by one element within $X$, remains accessible to $R$ despite another element of $X$ whose own default behaviour would have concealed it.
\end{schemabox}

The schema has three variables but five roles, and keeping them apart is what stops a case study from drifting. Applied to (\ref{ex:cgel-two-direction}a):

\begin{quote}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{0.66\linewidth}@{}}
\textit{Configuration} ($X$) & \mention{a number of spots}: a number-transparent quantificational noun plus its \mention{of}-complement \\
\textit{Property} ($P$)      & plural count profile \\
\textit{Bearer}              & \mention{spots}, the \mention{of}-complement \\
\textit{Mediator}            & \mention{number}, the morphologically singular head \\
\textit{Relation} ($R$)      & subject--verb agreement \\
\textit{Diagnostic}          & the number realized on \mention{have} \\
\textit{Concealing baseline} & head-driven agreement: \mention{the number of spots has appeared} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{quote}

Two of these are easy to run together and shouldn't be. The property is not its bearer: $P$ is the plural count profile, not the word \mention{spots}. And the relation is not the diagnostic that detects it: agreement is the relation, and verb morphology is how we see it. That second distinction does real work later, where a candidate relation turns out to be a diagnostic instead (§\ref{sec:6-korean}) or where evidence for accessibility exists without the relation having been specified (§\ref{sec:3}). The concealing baseline is what the schema's \mention{would have concealed} clause points to, and §\ref{sec:2-3} makes it the paper's falsification condition.

When is such a label worth having? The useful cases are the ones where it supports prediction; call these \term{projectibility profiles}. Projectibility inherits the question \textcite{Goodman1955} raises, of which predicates support reliable inductive projection; \textcite{boyd1991} supplies the stabilizer side with a maintenance-based realism. A profile has three components: an \term{observable base}, the predictions that base licenses, and a \term{stabilizing source}. The QN class shows the shape concretely. The base is membership in \textit{CGEL}'s small class. The predictions reach well beyond the agreement override in (\ref{ex:cgel-two-direction}): a noun in the class also shows a characteristic determiner frame (\mention{a lot of it} and totality \mention{the lot of it}, but not \ungram{\mention{some lot of it}}) and restrictions on its complement (\mention{a number of protesters} but not \ungram{\mention{a number of money}}), among the behaviours §\ref{sec:2} catalogues. The stabilizer is conventional lexical inheritance: the class is small and conventional, and membership carries the whole cluster as a package.

The substantive clause is \mention{remains accessible through $X$}. A case fits only if $X$ has internal structure whose default behaviour would have made $P$ inaccessible to $R$. Coordination (§\ref{sec:2-3}) is the textbook exclusion. In \mention{Bob and Jane are happy}, the plural agreement sums the conjuncts' features; no head's morphological number competes with them, so nothing was ever in a position to conceal $P$, and calling the construction \mention{transparent} would relabel summation as access. Everyday senses of \mention{transparent prose} or \mention{transparent intentions} are excluded too; the schema's slots have no values to bind.

This non-instance requirement is the umbrella's distinctive contribution. It keeps mediated accessibility from collapsing into a relabeling of familiar facts: the schema earns its place not by naming a kind but by supplying a relational diagnostic that a per-construction analysis lacks, one that separates genuine mediation from compositional summation and from everyday \term{clarity}. So \term{transparency}, as an umbrella, isn't itself projectible: applied on its own, the label projects nothing, and what projects is the particular profile it points to.

The paper therefore asks three questions. Which linguistic \mention{transparency} labels bind a non-trivial $X$-$P$-$R$ triple? Which triples support projection beyond the facts that motivated the label? What stabilizes the strongest profiles? The primary field is syntax, where the targets include agreement, syntactic-category licensing, complement selection, and behaviour under complement ellipsis. The answer is strongest for number-transparent QNs and transparent free relatives.

The English QN class illustrates the strongest case: once the class is recognized as small and conventional, membership predicts the whole cluster, not just the agreement override, reliably for core members and weakly at the edges (\mention{majority, minority}, sense-split \mention{couple}).

Transparent free relatives fill the slots differently. In \mention{What appear to be trousers are on the chair}, $X$ is the \mention{what}-clause, $P$ is the plural number profile borne by the nucleus \mention{trousers}, the mediator is \mention{what} (morphologically singular), and $R$ is matrix subject--verb agreement. In attributional cases, a second relation appears: the matrix accesses the nucleus's syntactic category through the \mention{what}-clause.

A typological extension (§\ref{sec:6}) then tests the topology of the schema beyond English: Spanish and Tsez keep the outward endpoint shape while Korean shifts it, since there the candidate property belongs to the mediator itself rather than to material inside it, and the survey in Appendix~\ref{sec:B} varies the source and direction of access (Aleut, Lardil, Kayardild, Chamorro) and marks the boundary where a feature is relayed onto a head rather than accessed directly (Algonquian/Kutenai obviation).

Projectibility is field-relative: a profile supports induction for a field's purposes, not for every inquiry at once. $X$ can be a lexical item, construction, compound, intensional context, or derivationally complex word. $P$ can be a number/count feature, constituent meaning, referent, or stem representation. $R$ can be agreement, interpretation, substitution under co-reference, or masked priming. Accessibility itself can be class-like, as in core QNs, or gradient, as in compositionality ratings and priming magnitudes.

Our aim is constructive rather than exhaustive. The paper leaves aside most uses of \term{transparency} in linguistics, including extraction, islands, barriers, and phases; extraction-transparency is acknowledged in §\ref{sec:2} but not developed, and Chamorro wh-agreement appears in §\ref{sec:6} only as a topology comparison. It also leaves aside phonological or orthographic transparency, everyday talk of \mention{transparent explanation}, and referential or \mention{de re}/\mention{de dicto} transparency, where the mediator is an intensional context rather than a form with internal structure and the schema's \mention{through $X$} clause has nothing to bind to.

\term{Mediated accessibility} is more specific than \term{clarity} and broader than \term{compositionality}. Clarity is rhetorical: it doesn't single out a mediator or relation. Compositionality is the limiting case of form--meaning transparency, where a mediator's parts predict the whole. Number transparency is different: a QN's morphological number doesn't by itself predict the verb's number, and under the construction it doesn't have to.

Section 2 takes up morphosyntactic transparency, with number-transparent QNs and transparent free relatives as paradigms. Section 3 turns to form--meaning accessibility (constructional compositionality \autocite{goldberg-2006-constructions-at-work}, rated compound transparency \autocite{bell-schafer-2016-modelling-transparency}, and time-sensitive lexical processing \autocite{heyer-kornishova-2018-priming-eventually}), where the relation $R$ shifts from agreement to interpretation and processing, and notes referential transparency as the schema's boundary case; that family is developed in its own right in separate work.

Section 4 applies Haspelmath's \autocite*{haspelmath-2010-comparative-concepts} comparative-concept discipline to the schema's cross-linguistic ambitions and introduces the topology map, with Korean dative subjects as the case the procedure adjudicates rather than certifies and the fuller Korean battery left as the central open test. Section 5 names the stabilizers and treats Slater's stable property cluster (SPC) framework \autocite{slater-2015-natural-kindness} and Khalidi's causal-network account \autocite{khalidi-2018-causal-networks} as complementary diagnostics.
