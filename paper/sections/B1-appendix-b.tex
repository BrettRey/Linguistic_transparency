\section{Accessibility topologies beyond the worked cases}\label{sec:B}

Spanish and Tsez (§\ref{sec:6-topologies}) and Korean (§\ref{sec:6-korean}) are the cross-linguistic cases the body of the paper works. This appendix sets out four further accessibility topologies the schema maps, each with the falsification condition that would retire it, plus a targeted check of head-marked systems. None of these is promoted to a worked projectibility profile; the point is to show the schema's reach and its edges. Every case remains subject to the §\ref{sec:2-3} default-conceal test: identify $X$, $P$, and $R$, then ask what same-language pattern would make the proposed access disappear.

\subsection{Aleut: null-source subpart access}\label{sec:B-aleut}

Aleut deserves separate mention because it changes the earlier head-marked caution. Merchant's missing-possessor contrast shows that when the possessor is overt, Aleut has ordinary agreement; when that possessor is unpronounced, the subject appears in relative case and the verb takes an anaphoric suffix \autocite[195]{merchant-2011-aleut-case-matters}.

\ea\label{ex:aleut-possessor}
\ea \gll Piitra-x̂ hla-s ada-a kidu-ku-x̂. \\
Peter-\textsc{abs} boy-\textsc{pl} father-\textsc{a:3s.abs} help-\textsc{pres}-\textsc{3s} \\
\glt `Peter is helping the boys' father.' (overt possessor: subject absolutive, ordinary agreement)
\ex \gll Piitra-m ada-a kidu-ku-u. \\
Peter-\textsc{rel} father-\textsc{a:3s.abs} help-\textsc{pres}-\textsc{a:3s/3s} \\
\glt `Peter is helping the boy's father.' (dropped possessor: subject in relative case, anaphoric verb suffix)
\z
\z

The controller is recoverable as a subpart of a non-subject NP, but it needn't be overt. In the schema's terms, $P$ can be null-source, provided the language supplies an independent contrast showing that $R$ tracks the missing subpart rather than the whole NP.

This is a positive case with a qualification. It differs from the simple overt-head-marking analogue of English QNs because Merchant's own analysis treats the missing argument as syntactically present pro moving into a local relation with T. The comparison therefore sharpens the typology rather than overturning the diagnostic: overt intact-NP transparency and null/anaphoric subpart accessibility are different topologies.

\subsection{Head-marked systems: possessors and the NP-internal question}\label{sec:B-head-marked}

A targeted check of head-marked systems adds the remaining caution. If English-style NP transparency were easy to replicate in head-marked grammar, the clearest cases should involve verbal indexing by an overt embedded nominal inside a larger NP while that nominal remains internal. The available positive candidates instead cluster around possessors, and most require a further analysis.

Chimane is the strongest near case: an apparently NP-internal possessor can control object agreement on the verb, but Ritchie's analyses treat the relation as mediated by applicative-like morphology and a clause-level proxy of the internal possessor \autocite{ritchie-2014-chimane-possessor-agreement,ritchie-2016-prominent-internal-possessors}. The minimal pair shows the mediation directly \autocite[622--623]{ritchie-2016-prominent-internal-possessors}.

\ea\label{ex:chimane-possessor}
\ea \gll Juan täj-je-' un mu' Sergio-s. \\
Juan(\textsc{m}) touch-\textsc{clf}-\textsc{3sg.f.o} hand(\textsc{f}) the.\textsc{m} Sergio(\textsc{m})-\textsc{f} \\
\glt `Juan touched Sergio's hand.' (default: the verb agrees with the feminine possessum \mention{hand})
\ex \gll Juan täj-je-bi-te un mu' Sergio-s. \\
Juan(\textsc{m}) touch-\textsc{clf}-\textsc{appl}-\textsc{3sg.m.o} hand(\textsc{f}) the.\textsc{m} Sergio(\textsc{m})-\textsc{f} \\
\glt `Juan touched Sergio's hand.' (the verb agrees with the masculine possessor \mention{Sergio}, licensed by the applicative \mention{-bi})
\z
\z

Other apparent positives are explicitly externalized: Nez Perce object possessor agreement is possessor raising, with the possessor moving from a possessum-DP-internal position to a clausal A-position \autocite{deal-2013-possessor-raising}; Deal's broader survey treats many comparable cases as external possession, possessor raising, incorporation, or related clause-level dependencies rather than as agreement with an embedded dependent inside an intact NP \autocite{deal-2017-external-possession}.

Yucatec Maya gives the useful negative backdrop: it's an exclusively head-marking language \autocite[51]{bohnemeyer-butler-jaeger-2015-yucatec-head-marking}, but in inalienable-possession predications the grammar consistently keeps possessor and possessum inside possessed NPs, apart from the existential possessor construction \autocite[113, 116, 127]{lehmann-2002-yucatec-possession}. The inalienable pattern is shown in (\ref{ex:yucatec-possession}), where the possessor \mention{le máak} `the person' stays inside the possessed subject NP \mention{u ho'l le máak} `the person's head' \autocite[110]{lehmann-2002-yucatec-possession}.

\ea\label{ex:yucatec-possession}
\gll chowak u ho'l le máak-e' \\
long \textsc{poss.3} head \textsc{def} person-\textsc{d3} \\
\glt `the person has long hair (lit.\ the person's head is long)'
\z

The diagnostic point is narrow: overt sensitivity to a possessor isn't enough. The decisive question is whether an embedded dependent remains NP-internal while controlling clause-level morphology.
