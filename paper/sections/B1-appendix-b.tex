\section{Accessibility topologies beyond the worked cases}\label{sec:B}

Spanish and Tsez (§\ref{sec:6-topologies}) and Korean (§\ref{sec:6-korean}) are the cross-linguistic cases the body of the paper works. This appendix sets out four further accessibility topologies the schema maps, each with the falsification condition that would retire it, plus two notes on what the table's entries do and don't establish. None is promoted to a worked projectibility profile; the point is to show the schema's reach and its edges, and to mark which entries lack a concealing control. Every case remains subject to the §\ref{sec:2-3} concealing-baseline test: identify $X$, $P$, and $R$, then ask what same-language pattern would make the proposed access disappear.

\subsection{Two notes on the table}

\textbf{Aleut} is the qualified positive. Merchant's missing-possessor contrast shows that with an overt possessor Aleut has ordinary agreement, while with an unpronounced one the subject appears in relative case and the verb takes an anaphoric suffix \autocite[195]{merchant-2011-aleut-case-matters}. So the bearer of $P$ can be recoverable as a subpart of a non-subject NP without being overt. The qualification is that Merchant analyses the missing argument as syntactically present \mention{pro} moving into a local relation with T, which keeps the case distinct from overt intact-NP transparency rather than overturning the diagnostic.

\textbf{The intact-NP question} is what the head-marked survey turns on. If English-style NP transparency were easy to replicate in head-marked grammar, the clearest cases should involve verbal indexing by an overt embedded nominal that stays NP-internal. The available candidates cluster around possessors and mostly require a further analysis: Chimane possessor agreement is mediated by applicative-like morphology and a clause-level proxy \autocite{ritchie-2014-chimane-possessor-agreement,ritchie-2016-prominent-internal-possessors}; Nez Perce object possessor agreement is possessor raising \autocite{deal-2013-possessor-raising}; and Deal's broader survey treats many comparable cases as external possession or incorporation rather than agreement with a dependent inside an intact NP \autocite{deal-2017-external-possession}. Yucatec Maya is the useful backdrop: an exclusively head-marking language \autocite[51]{bohnemeyer-butler-jaeger-2015-yucatec-head-marking} that nonetheless keeps possessor and possessum inside possessed NPs in inalienable predications \autocite[110, 113, 116, 127]{lehmann-2002-yucatec-possession}. The diagnostic point is narrow: overt sensitivity to a possessor isn't enough, and the decisive question is whether an embedded dependent remains NP-internal while controlling clause-level morphology.

\textbf{Relay, and what separates it from access.} The table's last row treats obviation as a boundary case rather than an instance, and that needs a criterion, since the paper elsewhere allows a feature to reach $R$ across intervening structure. The criterion is whether $R$ relates to the bearer's own feature or to a distinct feature token computed on an intermediate element. Where the relation is one of identity or structure-sharing, so that what $R$ tracks just is the bearer's feature, the property remains accessible: that is the QN and TFR situation, on the index-agreement analysis of §\ref{sec:2-2} explicitly so. Where an intervening head acquires a feature of its own and $R$ relates to that, the bearer has been replaced rather than reached, and the case falls outside. Kutenai and Algonquian obviation is of the second kind: the predicate agrees with a value already carried by the possessed head.

That criterion is analysis-dependent, as §\ref{sec:3}'s framework-portability implies. An analysis on which the intermediate feature is shared rather than computed would move obviation inside, and the paper's verdict travels with the analysis rather than above it. Two consequences worth stating. Possessor raising and external possession are non-instances of \mention{intact-NP} access specifically, which is not the same as falling outside the umbrella; a clause-level construction could serve as the configuration and the schema would apply with different bindings. And the boundary here is a boundary of one topology, not of mediated accessibility as such.

Two entries in the table are candidate topologies rather than established instances. Lardil and Kayardild (inward dependent access) and Chamorro (path-distributed access) are given with falsification conditions but without the same-family concealing control that §\ref{sec:2-3} requires, so they mark where the schema could be applied rather than where it has been.
